**In the woods of New Hampshire, a nonprofit cemetery is using QField, QGIS and [QFieldCloud](https://qfield.cloud) to make sure every grave marked by a tree can be found again, by anyone, for as long as the forest stands.**

### The Challenge

Life Forest is a conservation cemetery. At its first location in Hillsboro, New Hampshire, trees take the place of headstones. Cremated remains are wrapped in a wool shroud and buried beneath a newly planted tree, and the tree becomes a living memorial that families can come back to. At a second location in Maine, burials follow a woodland trail, marked by a small marker in a natural setting rather than a memorial tree.

The idea came from co-founder Mel Bennett, who needed a place to bury her mother. Her mother had always wanted to rest beneath a dogwood tree, and Mel wanted a place that honoured that wish and that the family could return to, wherever life took them.

"I've always found cemeteries feel very sterile," says Colton Sawyer, who leads GIS and mapping for Life Forest. "This is a place that feels warm."

A forest is not a lawn with neat rows, though, and a cemetery still has to keep records. At Life Forest, every plot is **legally deeded to its purchaser with its GPS coordinates recorded in the deed.** That makes accuracy the whole job. The location on paper has to match the tree in the ground, and it has to stay that way.

"My job is making sure that onsite and online accuracy are connected," Colton says.

The land itself makes that harder. Plots are circles of seven or fifteen feet in diameter, sized to give each tree room for its canopy, because trees planted too close together end up tangling their branches. An arborist, whom the team calls the tree doctor, advises on which species belong where: a spruce among the existing evergreens, a maple out in the open. Stumps, rocks and soil conditions all affect where a plot can actually go.

Then Colton moved from New Hampshire to Colorado, a long way from the nearest plot.

### The Solution

Life Forest had looked at other open source options, including publishing QGIS maps to the web, but settled on QGIS on the desktop, QField in the field and QFieldCloud to connect the two. The team started using QField around the time Colton left New Hampshire, about two and a half years ago.

The workflow runs in both directions. On the ground, the land manager or the crew preparing a site walks an open area with QField and draws its boundary, adding photos and notes on the soil and what is growing there. That data syncs back through QFieldCloud to Colton in Colorado. Working in QGIS, he lays out where the new trees should go, based on the site data and the arborist's advice, and pushes the planned plots back out to the field.

The crew then navigates to each planned point in QField, measures out the spacing with tape measures, and places a physical marker. If they hit a problem, such as a stump where a tree was planned, they move the plot and record the change, so the map stays true to the ground.

That simplicity matters, because the people in the field are not GIS specialists. "Our land manager is not tech savvy, and that's okay. It's not their job," Colton says. "He can run an excavator a lot better than I could." What the land manager needs is to look at his iPad, see where he is, and find the marker. "I'm right here. I just need to scan this area in a 10 foot radius and I can find that marker where it should be. That is incredibly helpful."

Once plots are marked, families can visit the land or look at Life Forest's online maps and choose a spot. They do not need QField to find it later. The recorded coordinates will lead a visitor to the right area with an ordinary phone and a maps app, and from there it is a short walk among the tree markers, much like finding a name in a traditional cemetery.

### The Result

The combination of open source tools and remote field collection is what made the project workable for a young nonprofit. Colton learned GIS on commercial software, but for a small nonprofit cemetery, the licences were out of reach.

"For a young startup, a cemetery, a nonprofit, we had no way to budget for that," he says. "Having this open source product, and not having to be physically on the land to do everything, has been super helpful."

- **Remote by design:** the mapping lead works from Colorado while the burial sites are in New Hampshire and Maine, with field data flowing both ways through QFieldCloud.
- **Accurate records:** every plot is laid out, verified on the ground and recorded with the GPS coordinates that go into the deed.
- **Room to grow:** new sites can be set up without flying the mapping lead out for every step, which lets Life Forest serve families from across the United States who want a deeded, GPS-marked tree for their loved ones.

Those records matter well beyond the families who buy a plot. Through a guardianship program, Life Forest gives a resting place to people whose remains go unclaimed, those with no family to take them, or whose next of kin cannot be traced. Their locations are recorded just as carefully as any other burial, so if a family ever traces the chain back, there is a place to go. Some families have already found their relatives at Life Forest this way.

> "People hear about Life Forest and they want that deed recorded, GPS marked tree to bury their loved ones under. And they want to know that they can travel to New Hampshire or Maine and find it. QField has given us the opportunity to do that without me having to be physically there."
>
> Colton Sawyer, Life Forest

### A Forest That Stays a Forest

Life Forest is a nonprofit, and the land around the burial areas is held in conservation. In Hillsboro, the cemetery is surrounded by 80 acres of conservation land, and the site is designated as an arboretum, with its collections of trees mapped so visitors can seek out particular species. One area, the Evergreen Grove, follows the evergreens that have historically grown there.

The organisation's aim is to make a cemetery that welcomes people of every age. It has sponsored a children's book about grief, and it hosts events such as a candlelit New Year's Eve walk up to the brook.

"Death doesn't have to be scary. It doesn't have to be sterile," Colton says. "The forest is alive. You walk and you hear the birds, you see tracks from the deer or the bear. It feels different to have death almost celebrated for its life."

Behind every tree is a point on a map that has to be right. With QField, a small team spread across the country can keep it that way.
